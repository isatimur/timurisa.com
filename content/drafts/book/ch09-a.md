Count the AI seats and a company looks transformed. Now ask whether a single approval path has changed. Often it has not.

Chapter 9 of the book opens with this gap. Many adoption stories start small. Someone buys a seat. A few engineers get faster. A product manager drafts more quickly. Support summarizes tickets with less effort. These gains are real. They are also tool-use stories: local acceleration, not institutional redesign.

The chapter makes a different case. The biggest gains come from redesigning the organization so delegated machine work compounds instead of colliding.

## Scarcity moves upward

AI does more than speed up output. It changes where scarcity lives. Execution gets cheaper. Exploration gets faster. More people can create artifacts that once needed a specialist. One operator can direct several agents in parallel.

Then new bottlenecks appear. Review load rises. Duplicate work spreads. Private agent workflows drift away from shared priorities. Standards get harder to enforce informally. Managers can no longer assume the org chart shows who can produce what.

So scarcity moves upward. It moves from typing to judgment, from production labor to orchestration, and from drafting to deciding which draft deserves trust.

The chapter states the rule plainly. More output per person is not the same as more throughput. An organization can produce far more while moving slower. When generation is nearly free, value does not vanish. It relocates, from making things to deciding which of them deserve trust.

## Why access is not enough

A company can buy licenses and encourage experiments and still stay structurally unchanged. Individuals get a little faster. Decisions, approvals and responsibility flow through the same assumptions as before.

The chapter points to a threshold beyond access. Dan Shipper, who is building the AI-native company Every, puts it as a provocation: "There is a 10x difference between an organization where 90% of engineers use AI versus one where 100% do." The chapter reads the last ten percent as the point where compounding appears. Once use is pervasive, documentation quality matters more. Examples, standards, prompts and policies start to act as leverage.

## The company as a harness

Earlier in the book, a harness is the environment built around a model so its work can be trusted. At company scale, the same idea means standards, specs, permissions, review rules and approved workflows, all written down. Humans and agents then work inside them.

Management stops inspecting every artifact. It designs the environment in which good artifacts are more likely to appear.

The chapter defines an AI-native organization as one that reorganized itself so machine-amplified work becomes cumulative instead of chaotic. Heavy AI use alone does not qualify. The strongest companies will be the ones that learned to convert cheap generation into trusted throughput.

## Run the audit

The chapter ends its argument with a list: broader creation, tighter review, earlier alignment, stronger internal platforms, clearer authority boundaries, and operating models that treat delegated work as organizational material.

Read that list as an audit. For each item, ask one question. Does it exist as a written artifact that someone owns, or only as a habit in people's heads? A written rule compounds. A habit evaporates the moment its owner is busy.

Pick the item where the answer is "habit." Write it down this week, and give it an owner.
