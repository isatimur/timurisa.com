Many AI adoption stories start small. Someone buys a seat. A few engineers get faster. A product manager drafts more quickly. Support summarizes tickets with less effort. These gains are real, and sometimes valuable. They are also tool-use stories. They describe local acceleration, not institutional redesign.

In Chapter 9 of my book I make a different case. The biggest gains come from redesigning the organization so delegated machine work can compound instead of colliding.

## Scarcity moves upward

AI does not only speed up output. It reorganizes where scarcity lives. Execution gets cheaper. Exploration gets faster. More people can create artifacts that once needed a specialist. One operator can direct several agents in parallel.

Then new bottlenecks appear. Review load rises. Duplicate work spreads. Private agent workflows drift away from shared priorities. Standards become harder to enforce informally. Managers can no longer assume the org chart tells them who can produce what.

So scarcity moves upward: from typing to judgment, from production labor to orchestration, from drafting to deciding which draft deserves trust. The practical test is simple. Find the step where work now piles up waiting on a person. That queue marks where scarcity moved. It is almost never the keyboard.

## Seats are not a redesign

The easy mistake is to measure adoption by access. A company can buy licenses and encourage experiments and still stay structurally unchanged. Individuals get a little faster. Decisions, approvals and responsibility flow through the same assumptions as before. Count the seats and AI looks transformative. Ask whether a single approval path has changed, and often it has not.

The chapter also gives a rule that is easy to state and easy to forget. More output per person is not the same as more throughput. An organization can produce far more while moving slower. When generation is nearly free, value does not vanish. It relocates, from making things to deciding which of them deserve trust.

This is why I describe the company as a harness for its own agents. Earlier in the book, a harness is the environment built around a model so its work can be trusted. At company scale, the same idea means standards, specs, permissions, review rules and approved workflows that are written down. Humans and agents then work inside them. Management stops inspecting every artifact. It designs the environment in which good artifacts are more likely to appear.

The chapter defines an AI-native organization as a company that reorganized itself so machine-amplified work becomes cumulative instead of chaotic. Heavy AI use alone does not qualify. The strongest companies will be the ones that learned to convert cheap generation into trusted throughput.

## What to do with this

You can start without a reorganization. Pick one workflow where agents already produce work. Find the queue where that work waits on a person. Ask who owns that queue, what rule lets them safely skip some of the items, and whether that rule exists in writing or only in someone's head. A written rule compounds. A habit evaporates when its owner is busy.

That single question points at the real investment. It may be a review path, a standard, or a platform. It will rarely be a faster keyboard.
